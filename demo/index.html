Can a quantum computer predict the tide?

Twice a day the sea at San Francisco rises and falls, by up to about two metres, pulled by the Moon and the Sun. Because the pull repeats in regular rhythms, the tide is very predictable. That makes it a good test for a new kind of prediction model, one that runs on a quantum computer. This page shows how well these models do, compared with the methods oceanographers already use. You don't need any physics or quantum background.

The idea in three steps

  1. 1

    Describe each hour by five “tidal clocks”

    The tide is driven by a few repeating rhythms. The biggest comes from the Moon and repeats every 12 h 25 min; another comes from the Sun and repeats every 12 h; others repeat about once a day. Picture each rhythm as the hand of a clock. Five clock-hand positions describe any hour.

  2. 2

    Turn each clock hand into a qubit

    A qubit, the basic unit of a quantum computer, can be rotated like a dial. Each clock hand sets how far one qubit is rotated, so five qubits store one hour. Rotations wrap around naturally, just like the clocks do.

  3. 3

    Predict from similar moments

    To predict a new hour, the model asks how similar it is to each hour it has already seen, by comparing their qubit states. Hours whose clocks point the same way get more say. The prediction is a weighted blend of their water levels.

Every model on this page learned from 1000 hours of 2021–2022 and is tested on all of 2023, a year it never saw. The quantum models run on a simulated quantum computer, which gives the same answers a perfect, noise-free one would.

How close do the predictions get?

Each card shows one model's score for 2023. A score of 1 would be perfect; 0 means no better than always guessing the average water level. A score of 0.93 means the model captures about 93% of the tide's ups and downs.

The black line is what really happened. Click a name under the chart to show or hide that model, and hover to read the values. Move the slider to another week. Where every model misses in the same way, the cause is usually wind or a storm pushing water around, which nothing based on the clocks alone can foresee.

Who are the other models?
Quantum kernel
The quantum model described above: it predicts from similar moments, with similarity measured by a quantum computer.
Trainable quantum circuit
A different quantum model: a small quantum program with adjustable settings, tuned step by step until its output matches the training data, much like training a neural network.
Gaussian process
A standard machine-learning method on an ordinary computer. It also predicts from similar moments, using a different similarity measure.
UTide
The method oceanographers use: it fits a sum of regular waves, one per tidal rhythm.
NOAA forecast
The official forecast from the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, built from decades of data. It is shown for reference, not as a fair competitor.

Try it: train the quantum model yourself

Your browser can run the quantum model right here. Choose how many hours it learns from and how much it smooths, and see how the 2023 score changes. With 1000 hours, training takes about a second.

Things to try: drop to 50 hours and watch the score fall, because a handful of examples is not enough to learn how five rhythms combine. Then move the smoothing slider to either end and see how the prediction changes.

For the curious: how can a browser run a quantum model?

For this particular way of storing data in qubits, the similarity of two hours has an exact formula that an ordinary computer can evaluate quickly: similarity = ∏j cos²((φj − φ′j)/2), where φj are the five clock-hand angles of the two hours. It gives exactly the same numbers as simulating the quantum circuit (we checked: they agree to 15 decimal places). The model then solves a standard equation, called kernel ridge regression, to decide how much weight each training hour gets. The smoothing slider is that equation's regularization strength.

That a formula like this exists is an important point: it means this quantum model gives no speed-up over an ordinary computer. It is useful here to understand what quantum models are good at, not as a quantum advantage.

Why does the trainable quantum circuit lag behind?

Think of the tide as a piece of music made of a few notes, the tidal rhythms and their combinations. A small quantum circuit can only play certain notes. We worked out the best score anyone could reach using exactly those notes: the ceiling (striped bars).

The ceiling is as high as the best methods, so the circuit has all the notes it needs. Its lower score comes from learning how loudly to play each note: training is slow and gets stuck. Giving the circuit more layers adds notes the tide doesn't contain, and that makes learning harder still.

Model scores are averages over five different sets of 1000 training hours. The ceilings use all 17,520 hours of 2021–2022. The bars start at 0.80 so that the differences are visible.

So, is the quantum computer better?

Not here. On the tides, and on three other real-world problems in the full study (wind farms, football shots and galaxy distances), no quantum model beat the best ordinary methods. But the quantum model with the right way of storing data came close, and the reasons it does well or badly can be explained and measured. That is what makes it useful to study: knowing where a quantum model fits is the first step to finding problems where it might one day help.
